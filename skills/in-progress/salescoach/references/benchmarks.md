# Benchmarks — what to track, and which research does not transfer

Consult this before importing any technique or quoting any number. Most published sales research is drawn from deals ten to a hundred times larger than a founder's first ones, and the mismatch is not merely a matter of degree — several findings **reverse**.

---

## 1. ⭐ The scale-mismatch table

| Technique / finding | Where it comes from | Verdict for a founder's first customers |
|---|---|---|
| **Closing restraint** ("don't push, be a trusted advisor") | Rackham's *large*-sale findings | **REVERSES.** In small sales, more closing attempts = higher win rate. Ask directly and repeatedly. This is the most-misquoted finding in sales. |
| MEDDIC / MEDDPICC | Six-figure deals with a formal paper process | ❌ There is no paper process for a card payment. Use the Mom Test commitment ladder. |
| Mutual action plans | 6–10 stakeholders, multi-month cycles | ❌ Signals the deal is bigger than it is, which slows it down. Send a recap email with owners and dates. |
| Multi-threading to 4–17 contacts | Enterprise buying committees; the +130% figure is for deals over $50K | ❌ At a 15-person firm, emailing three people reads as spam. **You need two: the champion and whoever signs.** |
| The 5.4-stakeholder buying group | CEB, purchases large enough to require consensus | ❌ At a few hundred euros a month, one person decides. Buying group ≈ 1–2. |
| Challenger's six-step pitch | Complex sales (54% of high performers there; the spread **flattens** in transactional sales) | ⚠️ Keep the Warmer and the Reframe. Drop Rational Drowning and Emotional Impact. |
| SPIN's Implication stage | Inflating pain to exceed a large price tag | ⚠️ **Creates** an objection at low price — "if it's such a big problem, why is it so cheap?" Use one light implication question only. |
| ROI decks and calculators | Enterprise buyers who see them daily | ❌ Correlates with a **27% drop** in close rate. Buyer-stated arithmetic is different and fine. |
| 47-minute demos | Mid-market SaaS | ⚠️ Keep the **ratio** (product ≈ 20–30% of the call), cut the duration. Target 20–25 minutes. |
| 11–14 discovery questions | 30–60 minute enterprise calls | ⚠️ **6–9** on a 20-minute founder call. |
| 12–15 touches across 3 channels | SDR teams with SDR tooling | ⚠️ Costs more hours than the contract is worth. Five to seven touches, two channels. |
| Volume outbound (1,402 dials/meeting) | Quota-carrying SDRs | ❌ Not founder-viable. **Small hand-built lists reply ~3x better than big ones.** Volume is the enemy here. |
| Cold-email reply rate of 0.45% | Agencies running 7.5M sends | ❌ Wrong population. A founder writing individually is the 3–5%+ population. |
| "Sell the meeting, not the product" | Enterprise | ⚠️ At small ACV a ten-minute call *is* the sales cycle. |
| Bosmeny's 70/30 listen ratio | Discovery advice | 🚩 On a **cold call** the number is 55:45. Applying 70/30 there means dead air. |
| Viral loops, k-factor, referral programmes | Post-PMF products with volume | ❌ At ten customers "the loop" is the founder asking who else is on the team. |
| Sales yield > 1.0 | Requires a sales team as the denominator | ❌ Meaningless solo — the denominator is the founder's own unpaid time. |
| NRR / churn percentages | *"aren't necessarily statistically significant before Year 2-3 and before $1m-$2m in ARR"* | ❌ 1 of 10 churning is not "10% churn", it's one customer. |
| Growth **rate** % | PG's 5–7%/week was calibrated on companies with real transaction volume | ❌ Below ~20 accounts it's arithmetically unstable — 1→2 customers is +100%. **Count absolutes.** |
| Burn multiple, CAC, LTV, CAC payback | Need volume and real spend | ❌ *"Measure for sure, but what really matters in the early days is keeping the burn rate down."* |
| Ebsta/Pavilion 6.5-month cycles, 19% win rates | 655,000 opportunities, mid-market and enterprise | ❌ **Actively harmful.** Benchmarking against these makes a founder tolerate six-month cycles that should be thirty days. |

## 2. What a deal should cost in time

| ACV | Expected cycle |
|---|---|
| **< $2,000** | *"close on average within 14 days. Ideally, 1-2 call closes."* |
| **< $5,000** | *"close on average within 30 days"* |
| < $25,000 | within 90 days |
| > $100,000 | 3–9 months |

⚠️ These describe *"an established sales engine"*. Pre-PMF and founder-led, expect longer. **But if a €3,000 deal is taking 90+ days, that is diagnostic** — usually the founder is talking to someone without authority, or the pain isn't urgent enough to act on.

**If it takes more calls than that, the number to fix is the call COUNT, not the close rate.**

⚠️ **No credible SMB demo→close benchmark exists.** The figures dominating search results ("SMB demo-to-close 32%", "sales-led 20–30% median") come from AI-generated SEO aggregators citing each other. **Do not import one.** The founder will establish their own within ~20 demos and it will be worth more than any of these. The two defensible general numbers: average B2B **win rate 21%**, **close rate 29%**.

## 3. Is ten customers the right goal?

Yes, and it's well calibrated. From interviews with 20+ successful B2B founders: **"3–10 paid customers"** is the threshold most of them named as the moment they felt product-market fit. Persona and Zip both said *"felt PMF after first 10 customers."* Gong converted 11 of 12 design partners.

Median idea → PMF is about **two years**; *"Start to worry if over 2 years without PMF; seriously worry if over 3 years."* And notably: **no founder in that study named retention as their primary PMF signal.**

**⭐ Split the ten into affiliated and unaffiliated.** People who found the product on their own, with no prior relationship, are the ones that count:

> **10 unaffiliated conversions is a harbinger of SaaS success.**

Ten customers from the founder's own network proves much less. Track the split weekly. And record the demographics and lead channel of the first conversions — failing to is *"a wasted learning opportunity."*

Also: **"Free customers are not customers, they don't count."**

## 4. The growth canon, and where it stops applying

> "A good growth rate during YC is 5-7% a week. If you can hit 10% a week you're doing exceptionally well. If you can only manage 1%, it's a sign you haven't yet figured out what you're doing."

> "The best thing to measure the growth rate of is revenue. The next best, for startups that aren't charging initially, is active users."

— Paul Graham. Compounded: 5%/wk → 12.6x/yr · 7% → 33.7x · 10% → 142x.

**⚠️ Below ~10–20 accounts a weekly growth rate is meaningless.** Track the absolute count — accounts, seats, MRR added this week.

Lemkin's threshold makes the same point from the other side: *"if you aren't growing > 10% a month after $10k in MRR or so … then you don't yet have product-market fit."* At €250/account that's ~40 accounts — **so a founder chasing their first ten is explicitly below the level where the test applies.**

Reality check on the other end: median annual growth for private B2B SaaS is **22%** (bootstrapped **20%**), and **the median company under $1M ARR was flat year-over-year**, with a top quartile at 139%. Use PG as early-stage ambition and 20% as steady-state reality — both are true of different populations. Encouragingly, one survey of 1,000+ companies found *"overall average annual contract value (ACV) levels do not appear to have an overall correlation with growth rate"* — a low-ACV strategy isn't a growth ceiling.

## 5. Do things that don't scale

> "The most common unscalable thing founders have to do at the start is to recruit users manually."

> "You can't wait for users to come to you. You have to go out and get them."

> "Your first users should feel that signing up with you was one of the best choices they ever made."

> "It's not the product that should be insanely great, but the experience of being your user."

> "You can provide a level of service no big company can."

The B2B-specific instruction:

> "Sometimes we advise founders of B2B startups to take over-engagement to an extreme, and to pick a single user and act as if they were consultants building something just for that one user."

On narrow markets:

> "Sometimes the right unscalable trick is to focus on a deliberately narrow market. It's like keeping a fire contained at first to get it really hot before adding more logs."

And the one to read as self-diagnosis, which the coach should quote when the founder has been coding instead of calling:

> "One is a combination of shyness and laziness. They'd rather sit at home writing code than go out and talk to a bunch of strangers."

> "At least one founder (usually the CEO) will have to spend a lot of time on sales and marketing."

Stripe's version: *"When anyone agreed to try Stripe they'd say 'Right then, give me your laptop' and set them up on the spot."*

## 6. The uncomfortable test

> "If your friend was standing next to you and their hair was on fire, that fire would be the only thing they really cared about in this world."

> "You need to find problems so dire that users are willing try half-baked, v1, imperfect solutions."

— Michael Seibel

Signal to look for: *"a frantic founding team trying to deal with ever-growing numbers of happy, loyal, and ideally paying customers."* Not headcount, not funding.

**The weekly metric this implies: how many prospects this week described the pain unprompted, before the founder pitched?** For a category where the problem is widely acknowledged but rarely urgent, this test is genuinely uncomfortable and should be applied honestly.

## 7. Retention, at the right frequency

> "Weekly growth and Retention." / "Retention is the most important."

Identify the value metric, then the **natural frequency** at which it should occur, and measure retention at that cadence: Airbnb = bookings, annual · Instagram = active users, daily · Gusto = running payroll, monthly · a product used in recurring team meetings = **weekly**.

A good product shows *"a flat line of people using the product continuously every single week."*

**A weekly natural frequency is a structural advantage — it gives a clean retention read far faster than most businesses get one. Exploit it.** The metric is **weekly active team accounts**, not users.

⚠️ Lemkin says defer churn/NRR to $1–2M ARR; Alströmer says retention is the most important metric. Not a conflict: Alströmer means **usage** retention (observable and vital at ten accounts), Lemkin means **revenue** retention (statistically empty at ten accounts).

## 8. The 40% test

**Question:** *"How would you feel if you could no longer use the product?"* — Very disappointed / Somewhat disappointed / Not disappointed. **Threshold: ≥40% "very disappointed".**

> "Companies that struggled to find growth almost always had less than 40% of users respond 'very disappointed,' whereas companies with strong traction almost always exceeded that threshold"

Superhuman's real trajectory: **22% → 33%** after narrowing to the target user → **58%** after three quarters of work.

Their four questions:

1. "How would you feel if you could no longer use [Product]?"
2. "What type of people do you think would most benefit from [Product]?"
3. "What is the main benefit you receive from [Product]?"
4. "How can we improve [Product] for you?"

Their method: split the "somewhat disappointed" group by whether the core benefit resonated; attend closely to those it did, and deliberately disregard those it didn't.

⚠️ Results become *"directionally correct... around 40 respondents"*, and the original derivation could not be verified at primary source (secondary sources disagree on whether the base was ~50 or ~100 startups).

**At ten accounts: run questions 2–4 at seat level for qualitative signal.** Those three are the valuable part at this stage — they build the profile of the high-expectation customer. Treat 40% as directional, not a gate.

## 9. ⭐ The weekly dashboard

### The one number

**Paying team accounts.** An integer. Not seats, not signups, not a percentage.

### Lagging — record, don't optimise weekly

| Metric | Note |
|---|---|
| Paying accounts | split **affiliated vs unaffiliated** |
| MRR, and MRR added this week | absolute, not % |
| **Seats per account** | the ARPA lever; retention improves monotonically with ARPA |
| Accounts lost | a count, not a percentage |

### Leading — this is the actual week

| Metric | Benchmark to beat |
|---|---|
| New prospects contacted | keep lists under 50 per campaign (~3x reply rate) |
| Replies received | high single digits for individually written email |
| Conversations held | bounded by the real call-slot count in `product.md` |
| **Prospects who described the pain unprompted** | the hair-on-fire test |
| **Trials the founder personally emailed** | target **100%** — outreach roughly doubles trial conversion |
| **Trial accounts reaching ≥2 active seats** | activation and expansion are the same event |
| Deals in flight over 30 days | should have closed in 1–3 calls |
| **Weekly active team accounts** | the natural-frequency retention read |
| Week-2 and week-4 account activity | is the retention curve flattening? |

### Monthly, not weekly

The 40% survey (needs ~40 respondents) and the three qualitative questions behind it.

### Explicitly excluded

CAC · LTV · LTV:CAC · NRR% · burn multiple · CAC payback · cumulative charts of anything · signups, trials and downloads as headline numbers · free users · logo churn % · sales yield · weekly growth rate % · **and email open rate** (Apple Mail Privacy Protection pre-loads tracking pixels, which makes it uninterpretable).

> "Downloads (or number of apps delivered by distribution deals) are really just a vanity metric."

> "Cumulative charts by definition always go up and to the right for any business that is showing any kind of activity. But they are not a valid measure of growth—they can go up-and-to-the-right *even when* a business is shrinking."

> "Different companies have almost unlimited definitions for what 'active' means... Be clear on how you define 'active.'"

— a16z

And: *"Don't get lost in secondary metrics and miss the bigger early-stage goals."* The three questions that matter — do you have PMF so you can grow fast enough; do your customers love you enough to beget you more customers; will you run out of money before escape velocity.

## 10. Claims to refuse

Never repeat these as fact. If the founder brings one, say where it comes from.

- *"80% of successful sales take five or more follow-ups"* and *"60% of customers reject an offer four times before buying"* — Marketing Donut / "National Sales Executive Association" lineage; never substantiated.
- *"44% of salespeople stop after one try"* — same lineage.
- *"Multi-threading lifts win rates 34%"* — it's from a single customer case study where multi-threading was one of several changes, not a controlled measurement.
- *"Deals with a mutual action plan close X% more often"* — no such study exists at Gong, Dock or Accord.
- *"97% close rate for prospects who viewed the deal room 12+ times"* — a selection-effect artefact.
- *"Groove's founder email got a 41% reply rate"* — unverifiable; the source blog no longer exists.
- *"Users who reach the aha moment convert at 3–5x"*, *"aha must happen within 18 minutes"*, *"behaviour-triggered emails convert 3.8x better per Intercom's SaaS engagement study"*, *"activation optimisers see 2.4x higher trial conversion"* — all trace to AI-generated SEO content citing each other. **The Intercom study does not appear to exist.**
- *"Roughly 30% of customers take a trial extension when offered"* — no primary source.
- *"Gartner: shorter trials outperform longer by 20%"* — no retrievable Gartner report.
- Any LinkedIn connection-accept or InMail response benchmark — no credible dataset was found. **Measure the founder's own from week one.**
- *"'How've you been?' converts 6.6x on cold calls"* — real, but from a 90k-call study superseded by a 300M-call one.

**And the general rule, which is the right conclusion given a documented 45x spread in published cold-email reply rates and a 10x spread in trial conversion:**

> "The trick to establishing benchmarks does not concern someone else's metrics."

Work backwards from the founder's own target through their own conversion rates. Replace each imported benchmark with their own number as soon as there are 20+ observations of it.
