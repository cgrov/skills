## 1. Discovery — The Mom Test

The most transferable body of work here, because it was written for founders with no sales training talking to real users about small money.

**The three rules, verbatim (Rob Fitzpatrick):**

> "1. Talk about their life instead of your idea.
> 2. Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future.
> 3. Talk less and listen more."

### Good questions — verbatim, use this wording

1. "Why do you bother?"
2. "What are the implications of that?"
3. **"Talk me through the last time that happened."**
4. "Talk me through your workflow."
5. "What else have you tried?"
6. **"How are you dealing with it now?"**
7. "Where does the money come from?"
8. "Who else should I talk to?"
9. "Is there anything else I should have asked?"

The two hardest-working: **"Talk me through the last time that happened"** gets into day-to-day detail; **"How are you dealing with it now?"** reveals how much money and effort they already spend on the problem — an existing budget line.

The last two close every conversation and must not be skipped. "Who else should I talk to?" is the referral ask *and* a test of reputation currency.

### Bad questions — verbatim, never ask these

1. "Do you think it's a good idea?"
2. "Would you buy a product which did X?"
3. "How much would you pay for X?"
4. "What would your dream product do?"
5. "Would you pay X for a product which did Y?"

Every one solicits an opinion about a hypothetical future instead of evidence from the actual past. Every one produces a false positive.

### The three types of bad data: compliments, fluff, ideas

> "Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning: shiny, distracting, and worthless."

**Fluff** has three grammatical tells:

> "Generic claims ('I usually', 'I always', 'I never'), Future-tense promises ('I would', 'I will'), Hypothetical maybes ('I might', 'I could')"

**Operational rule for the coach:** the moment the founder reports "they said they'd definitely use it", tell them they collected zero data, and give them the anchor question for next time — *"When did you last look for something like this?"*

### ⭐ Commitment and advancement — the pass/fail for every conversation

> "Commitment — they are showing they're serious by giving up something they value such as time, reputation, or money."

| Currency | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Time** | A scheduled next meeting with known goals; sitting through a trial; giving real feedback |
| **Reputation** | An intro to peers, their team, or their boss — *"I believe in this enough to risk looking stupid if it doesn't work out"* |
| **Money** | Deposit, pre-order, first month billed |

**If the founder got none of the three, the meeting failed.** Say it in those words. At small ACV the money rung is cheap enough to reach for far earlier than the book's enterprise readers can — skip letters of intent and go straight to a paid first month.

## 2. Discovery mechanics (Gong)

⚠️ Gong's own numbers are inconsistent across articles (43:57 vs 46:54 talk ratio). Different corpora and dates. Use the direction.

- **11–14 targeted questions** on a full-length enterprise discovery call (n=519,000 calls); beyond 14 it *"will likely start to feel like an interrogation, rather than a natural conversation."*
- **Spread questions evenly.** Average performers front-load them, *"working through checklists rather than having natural conversations."*
- **Explore 3–4 problems, no more.** *"Looking at more than that makes all the problems seem less urgent."*
- Talk:listen ~**45% you / 55% them** on discovery.
- **Slides on a discovery call cut follow-up booking by 17%.**
- **Company overview: cap at 2 minutes.** *"After that, there is a sharp dropoff in win-rates."*
- **Discuss competitors early → 49% likelier to close.**
- **Social proof lowers close rate 22%, and 47% on early-stage calls.**
- **No next steps discussed on the first call → close rates drop 71%.**
- **SMB deals without a decision-maker on the call: 80% less likely to close.**
- Scheduling ⚠️: best show rates ~3pm; Monday highest attendance; avoid Fridays.

**⚠️ Scale down the count, keep the ratio.** 11–14 questions comes from 30–60 minute enterprise calls. On a 20-minute founder call, **6–9 targeted questions** is the honest analogue, and 2–3 problems rather than 3–4.

⚠️ **Not published, do not invent:** a Gong question-to-statement ratio, a "longest customer story" target duration, or any research about "did you know" moments in demos. If you've seen these attributed to Gong, they're fabricated.

### Gong's own discovery questions, verbatim, by stage

*Latent pain* — "What's your biggest strategic priority for this year?" · "What initiatives are you rolling out as part of your plan?" · "Where are you in the process?"

*Active pain* — "How is this presenting itself as a problem to the business?" · "How are you currently addressing [pain]?" · "Where does solving [challenge] sit on your priority list?"

*Solutions development* — "What would happen if we solved [challenge]?" · "What happens if we don't address this now?" · "What is your [boss/CEO] looking for in this area of the business?"

*Decision* — **"Who would feel left out if they didn't attend the demo?"** · "Who else will these changes affect?"

That bolded one is the cheapest, least threatening multi-stakeholder question in the literature, and it's ideal for a seat-based product where you need several people to actually adopt.

## 3. SPIN, and the finding everyone gets backwards

Rackham's base: **35,000+ calls, 10,000 salespeople, 23 countries, 12 years.**

### ⭐ The small-vs-large sales finding

Rackham did **not** find that closing techniques don't work. He found they work in small sales and backfire in large ones:

- **Small sales** (one-call, low price, low risk): **more closing attempts = higher win rate.**
- **Large sales**: more closes = *lower* win rate; top performers used **20–30% fewer closes**.

> **A founder selling a few hundred euros a month to a small firm is on the small-sales side of the line.** Asking directly for the business, proposing the start date, sending the payment link unprompted — Rackham's own data says these help. The entire modern "don't close, just be a trusted advisor" orthodoxy derives from his *large*-sale findings and does not apply. This is the single most important correction in the whole corpus, and it is the one most often quoted in reverse.

⚠️ Honesty flag: the primary text of Chapter 2 could not be retrieved; all sourcing for this is secondary summaries. The **direction** is very well attested. **Do not attach a specific percentage to the small-sales side** — the numbers circulating with it are not traceable.

### The four question types

**S — Situation** (most over-used, least correlated with success — and today the answers are online, so pre-answer them from research): "What does your current workflow for [X] look like?"

**P — Problem**: "What's the most frustrating part of that process right now?" · "Where are things starting to break down as your team scales?"

**I — Implication**: "How does that delay affect your quarterly targets?" · "What's the cost of that inefficiency over 6-12 months?"

**N — Need-payoff** (makes *them* articulate the benefit): "What would it mean for your team if that bottleneck disappeared?" · "How would it change things if you recovered 8-10 hours weekly?"

**⚠️ Implication is where SPIN goes wrong at small ACV.** The Implication stage exists to inflate perceived pain until it exceeds a large price tag. When the price is below the annoyance threshold, a heavy Implication sequence reads as manipulative theatre and *creates* an objection — "if this is such a big problem, why is it so cheap?"

**Run S (1–2, pre-answered) → P (2–3) → N (1), and largely skip I.** Use one light implication question, only to establish the pain recurs:

> "How often does that come up — every meeting, or now and then?"

Save the Implication machinery for expansion conversations, where the seat arithmetic actually starts to matter.

## 4. Qualification frames

> "If your motion is short-cycle, single-buyer, and sub-$10K, MEDDIC is the wrong tool."

> "A $12K self-serve expansion and a $400K enterprise platform sale getting the same qualification checklist creates the predictable result: reps either over-qualify simple deals with unnecessary rigor or under-qualify complex ones by checking four boxes and calling it done."

| Frame | Verdict at small ACV |
|---|---|
| MEDDIC / MEDDPICC | ❌ Built for six-figure deals with a formal paper process. There is no paper process for a card payment. |
| BANT | ⚠️ Closest classic, but "Budget" is near-meaningless — nobody has a budget line, and asking implies a bigger purchase than it is. |
| ANUM (Authority, Need, Urgency, Money) | ✅ Better — leads with Authority, treats Money last. |
| Mom Test commitment ladder | ✅✅ Best fit — a pass/fail on time, reputation or money. |

**Four questions that replace MEDDIC's twenty:**

1. Who else on the team would be in this? *(seat count = deal size; the only real budget question)*
2. Can you turn it on for your team without asking anyone? *(authority)*
3. What triggered you looking now? *(urgency, critical event)*
4. What are you doing instead today? *(need; Mom-Test compliant; reveals switching cost)*

## 5. The friendly contact who isn't the buyer

From *The Challenger Customer*, verbatim descriptions:

**Mobilizers** — **Go-Getter** *"adeptly drives change because they want to champion ideas that generate business results"* · **Teacher** *"effectively paints a vision that builds momentum for new ideas"* · **Skeptic** *"carefully examines the details and then pushes for change in bite-sized increments"*

**Talkers** — **Friend** *"Though this person always takes your call, they rarely buy"* · **Guide** *"shares information but lacks the influence to drive actual change"* · **Climber** *"backs out at the first sign of reputational risk"* 🚩 (the book files Climber as a Talker; Challenger Inc's current site reclassifies it as a Blocker)

**Blocker** — *"believes stability is the goal and casts a wary eye at any change."*

> "The guide who provides information, the friend who enjoys social relationships with suppliers and the climber who looks for the personal benefit of every deal, are 'talkers.' They can talk about their support of the sale, but they are unable to deliver a consensus."

Sellers who target Mobilizers are **31% more likely to be high performers.**

### ❌ Mostly does not transfer — but one idea does

The 5.4-stakeholder buying group (CEB, n=3,000; with one decision maker purchase probability is 81%, with six it's 31%) is an artefact of purchases large enough to require consensus. ⚠️ It's also a decade old; successor research says ~11. At a few hundred euros a month a partner or team lead simply decides. Buying group ≈ 1–2.

🚩 And the caveat popular retellings drop: **the Challenger advantage is concentrated in complex sales** (54% of high performers there; the spread flattens in transactional sales, where the Relationship Builder penalty largely disappears). That's why Challenger is a bad *default* at this scale.

**What does transfer: reframe the profiles as an ADOPTION filter, not a purchasing filter.** For a team product the buying group is small but the *adoption* group is the seat count. The founder's version of the Talker problem isn't "my champion can't build consensus for a €200k purchase" — it's **"this enthusiastic individual cannot get the other five people in the room to change how the meeting runs."**

- **Your Go-Getter** = the person who already runs the meeting and controls its format. They can change team behaviour unilaterally. This is who you want.
- **Your Friend / Guide** = the delightful individual who loves the product, takes every call, and has no authority over how their team works. They will not convert a team account.

**The diagnostic question** — phrase it this way, not "are you the decision maker?", which makes people posture:

> "Who decides how your team's meetings run today — is that you?"

or, for power:

> "If this works for the three of you, who says yes to putting the whole team on it?"

## 6. Challenger's pitch — keep two steps, drop four

The six-step commercial teaching sequence, verbatim:

1. **The Warmer** — *"present your assessment of the key issues facing the customer, based on what you've seen at similar companies."* Lead with understanding, not questions.
2. **The Reframe** — *"you offer a new insight that connects them to a bigger problem or a bigger opportunity."*
3. **Rational Drowning** — *"make the business case… Quantify the cost or opportunity with data and charts."* Presented *"specifically to make them squirm."*
4. **Emotional Impact** — *"Describe how other companies suffered from doing what the customer is doing."*
5. **A New Way** — *"Review step-by-step the capabilities the customer would have to have in order to solve the problem."* Vendor-agnostic. The book warns: *"It's tempting, at this point, to launch into your solution."* Don't.
6. **Your Solution** — *"demonstrate that your solution is the answer."*

Organising question: *"What's currently costing our customers more money than they realize, that only we can help fix?"*

**⚠️ At small ACV: keep 1 and 2, drop 3–5, compress 6.** Running Rational Drowning about a few hundred euros a month reads as absurd. The Warmer takes ~45 seconds and the Reframe ~30 — one genuine non-obvious observation about how the problem actually fails, which is the founder's real differentiation as someone who studied it.

## 7. Demos

### The data (Gong)

- Successful demos average **47 min** vs unsuccessful 36. ⚠️ Gong's own reading is explicitly non-causal: *"More likely, the duration is due to the sales demo being so targeted and resonant, it stimulates a robust conversation."* **Never pad a demo to hit a length.**
- **The product-presentation segment: winning 9.1 minutes, losing 11.4.** So a winning 47-minute call is ~9 minutes of product and ~38 of conversation. **That ratio is what to copy, not the duration.**
- **⭐ Feature-dumping: win rate 26% when it starts at the very beginning of a demo, falling to 5% at 500+ seconds in.** Top reps discuss features **39% less** and spend **52% more time on business topics**.
- **⭐ The 76-second ceiling:** *"We didn't find a single demo that lead to a closed deal in the analysis that involved more than 76 seconds of interrupted pitching."* This is the most actionable number in the corpus. It costs nothing, requires no methodology, and scales to every deal size.
- **Order features by the most-discussed problem FIRST**, not last — mirror the discovery call, don't save the best for the end.
- Successful demos have **21% more speaker switches per minute** and **36% more in the second half** — the conversation should accelerate, not wind down.
- Top performers receive **~⅓ more questions from buyers** and ask **30% fewer** during the demo.
- **Next steps: winning demos give it 12.7% more time (~4 minutes).** No next steps → close rates drop **71%**.
- **ROI proof: *"Attempting to prove ROI at any point in the sales process correlates with a 27% drop in your likelihood of closing a deal."*** This is a gift at small ACV — don't build a calculator.
- Objections: top reps respond **with a question** 54.3% of the time vs 31% for average reps ⚠️ (could not be re-confirmed on the source page — treat as directional).

### Two practitioner ideas worth stealing

**The Buy-In Summary** — before sharing your screen, play back their workflow, pains, desired outcome and urgency, and **explicitly ask them to confirm it**. Everything after that is answering a question they've just agreed they have.

**The four-beat loop, per feature** — re-anchor to the pain → show the capability → one short customer story → ask an engagement question. Repeat. It's what keeps the speaker-switch rate up.

And the failure mode, in Kazanjy's words: *"show and tell (or better, 'show up and throw up') presentation."* His fix: *"Skip over features in your demo, that you don't think will appeal to the audience."*

**⭐ On demo data quality — the most useful line for a founder demoing their own product:**

> "Any time you find yourself saying things like 'Well, ignore that' or 'Imagine if XYZ,' you probably have a data problem."

Never say "ignore that". Fix the demo instance, or use the prospect's real data.

### ⭐ The 22-minute demo — scaled for a small deal

| Min | Segment | Grounding |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | **Time check + agenda.** "I've got you for 20 — I'll ask a few things first, show you two things, and tell you the price. Sound right?" | agenda-setting |
| 1–3 | **Warmer.** A researched hypothesis about how their version of the problem fails. A statement, not a question. | Challenger 1; Kazanjy |
| 3–10 | **Discovery: 6–9 Mom-Test questions, 2–3 problems.** Spread them. **No slides.** | Gong scaled; slides −17% |
| 10–11 | **Buy-In Summary.** Play back, get confirmation. | Estner |
| 11–18 | **Product: ~7 min, TWO features, most-discussed problem first.** Four-beat loop each. **No monologue over 60 seconds.** Real data only. | 9.1-min rule; 76s; 26%→5% |
| 18–19 | **Price, plainly.** ~¾ of the way through. Never say "list price". | Gong pricing window |
| 19–20 | **Trial close.** | Kazanjy |
| 20–22 | **Next steps, calendar invite sent before hanging up.** | −71% without |

**Do not include:** an ROI calculation (−27%), logo social proof early (−47%), a slide deck during discovery (−17%), a feature tour (26%→5%), Rational Drowning, Emotional Impact.

**Kazanjy's trial close, verbatim** — use when the prospect hasn't asked about price:

> "Given all we've covered, is this something that you see being useful in solving your [problem] challenges?"

"No" → dig into the objection immediately. "Yes" or "depends on pricing" → go to price.

## 8. Pilots

### Charge something, or it isn't a pilot

> "If you don't charge, it's not a pilot. It's an extended demo."
> "I almost never see 'Free Pilots' convert to paid. They are usually a sign of desperation in sales."
> "The feedback you get is often radically different from free 'customers' than paying customers."
> — Jason Lemkin

> "Until you've got a prospect to actually pay for something, assume they're lying and won't." — Sterling Snow (Divvy), via Lemkin

Lemkin's paid-pilot → annual conversion, from his own portfolio experience ⚠️ (not a controlled study): *"roughly, the conversions vary fairly widely from about 60% to over 90%"*. And: *"The more business process change involved, the lower the conversion rate likely will be."*

**⚠️ At small ACV a separate pilot fee is nonsense** — it would be most of the contract. **The lightweight equivalent: skip the pilot fee and bill the first month.** "€X/seat, N seats, first month billed now, cancel by [date] and I refund it" achieves what Lemkin wants — money attached, honest feedback, real deployment — without inventing a pilot SKU.

### Pilot purgatory, and how to avoid it

> "not setting the right expectations for a pilot with the customer and poor pilot execution, are the most common reasons as to why pilots fail to convert to a purchase."

> "Pilots convert to customers when stakeholders (not your pilot POC in most cases) agree to purchase your product, provided specific conditions are met during the trial."

Named causes of failure: free pilots · unclear success metrics · undefined timelines · scope creep and custom work · inadequate customer resource commitment · missing stakeholder alignment.

### The seven things a pilot must define

> use case / the business problem / the stakeholders / the data or workflow needed / the timeline / the success criteria / **the decision at the end**

And the governing rule:

> "choose the lightest offer that can produce credible signal."

At small ACV that's a **30-day paid team pilot with two check-ins**, not a 60-day structured POC.

### Language for setting one up

*The following are constructed from the components above, not quotations. Adapt the wording; keep the structure.*

**Proposing it:**

> "Here's what I'd suggest. Rather than you evaluating this in the abstract, let's run it properly with one team for four weeks. I'll set it up, I'll do the onboarding session myself, and we'll look at it together on [date]. It's €X for the month — I'd rather you paid, because free trials get ignored and I want this to actually get used. If it hasn't earned its place by [date], we stop and I refund it."

**Defining success criteria — ask, don't assert:**

> "Before we start — what would have to be true on [date] for you to say this worked? Not 'we liked it'. Something you'd actually notice."

Then write it back to them: *"So: by [date], [specific observable outcome], across [N people]. If we hit that, we continue. If we don't, we don't."*

**Setting the decision date, at the start and not the end:**

> "Let's put the decision meeting in the calendar now. [Date], 20 minutes, you and me. If it's a yes we do the paperwork on that call. If it's a no, you tell me why and I'll stop chasing you. Does that work?"

**⭐ The pre-close — the highest-leverage question in this document. Ask it BEFORE the pilot starts:**

> "Assume for a second that on [date] it's obviously working and your team doesn't want to give it up. What actually happens then? Who signs? Do you need anyone else in the room? Is there a purchasing step, or is it your call?"

That does the work of an entire MEDDIC discovery in one sentence, and it's how you satisfy the "80% less likely to close without a decision-maker" finding without a multi-threading campaign.

### Design partners are a different thing — don't blur them

The standard design-partner agreement's commitment checkboxes, verbatim — **this is the menu of things you can trade for price**:

- *"Participate in [#] Feedback sessions per [timeframe]"*
- *"Provide case study that can be shared with others"*
- *"Appear as a customer in private customer lists"*
- *"Appear as a customer on Provider's website and public customer lists"*
- *"Serve as a reference for prospective customers"*

⚠️ **75% of design partner agreements include no fees**; nearly half run 3 or 6 months.

🚩 That looks like it contradicts Lemkin's "free pilots never convert" — it doesn't. A **design partner** is a product-development relationship (free access buys their feedback); a **pilot** is a sales-evaluation relationship (they're buying an outcome). **For the first ten paying accounts the design-partner motion produces logos, not revenue.** Use it for at most two of the ten, and make the checkbox list an explicit trade.

## 9. Closing

### Hold two findings at once

**Rackham:** in small sales, ask — repeatedly and directly.

**Dixon (JOLT, 2.5M recorded conversations):** **40–60% of qualified pipeline is lost to no decision**, not to a competitor. Of those, 44% preferred the status quo and 56% were paralysed by risk. And crucially: **dialling up FOMO backfired 87% of the time.**

**The reconciliation: make a direct, confident recommendation with the risk removed — not pressure.**

> ✅ "I'd start with the twelve-person team, month to month, cancel any time — here's the link."
> ❌ "This price expires Friday."

**JOLT, verbatim:**

> **Judge** — "Start by assessing your customer's level of indecision… if they are overwhelmed by choices, still in the research phase, or concerned about the risk involved in the purchase."
> **Offer** — "offer them a recommendation tailored to their situation."
> **Limit** — "Encourage your customers to stop endless research and start trusting you as the expert."
> **Take risk off the table** — "(1) proactively resetting expectations, not letting the customer anchor on the most spectacular case studies, and (2) establishing 'safety net' options like implementation support, specialized terms, and close collaboration."

And the technique for surfacing an unspoken blocker:

> "Send out 'pings' by articulating potential issues or questions and listen for 'echoes,' the customer's response or confirmation. This opens the door to an honest conversation."

The posture shift is from *"What are you in the mood for?"* to a recommendation — you **"share the burden of a potentially bad decision."**

### Ask, in these words (Kazanjy, verbatim)

> "Based on what we've discussed, this sounds like a great fit. Is this something that you would want to progress with?"

> "Is there anything that is preventing us from getting you started as a customer?"

> "directly asking for the sale (compared to beating around the bush, or avoiding it because you're afraid of rejection) is 90% of the battle."

And the phrase Gong's data singles out — top performers use **"fair"** an average of **1.7x per deal** vs **0.2x** for everyone else:

> "All I ask is if it makes sense—if you're intrigued by what you learn—that we schedule a concrete next step. Is that fair?"

### Next steps

> "make sure that you always have the next meeting on the calendar" — Kazanjy

*"In the fastest deals, the seller spent 53% more time discussing 'next steps' during the first meeting."* A **specific** CTA naming a day and time books within 10 days **37%** of the time vs 32% open-ended.

🚩 **This reverses on cold outreach**, where interest-based CTAs win (30% vs 15%). "Be specific" applies *after* interest is established. Getting this backwards is a common misreading.

### ❌ Skip the mutual action plan — send a recap email instead

The MAP evidence is much weaker than its circulation suggests. No controlled statistic exists from Gong, Dock or Accord. Dock's *"97% close rate for prospects who viewed the deal room 12+ times"* is a **selection-effect artefact** — people who visit twelve times were already going to buy. The circulating "deals with a MAP close X% more often" is **commonly repeated, unverified.** MAPs earn their keep at 6–10 stakeholders over multi-month cycles.

At small ACV a five-section document with a procurement phase and a security-review checklist signals that you think this is a bigger deal than it is — which invites the buyer to treat it as one, which slows it down.

**⭐ The version that keeps 90% of the value and takes 90 seconds:**

> "Quick recap so we're on the same page:
> **What we agreed matters:** [their success criterion, in their words]
> **You:** pick the 8 people for the pilot — by Thursday
> **Me:** set up the workspace and run the 30-min onboarding — Monday 10:00
> **Us:** decision call — [date], 20 min. If it's working we start the paid plan that day.
> Shout if I've got any of that wrong."

Stakeholders, owners, dates, success criteria, decision date. That *is* a mutual action plan.

### Urgency without gimmicks — cost of inaction

> "True urgency isn't created through artificial deadlines or pressure tactics. It emerges naturally when prospects fully understand the consequences of maintaining the status quo."

Loss-framing **the status quo** works. Loss-framing **your offer** doesn't (Dixon: 87% backfire). Where the buyer bills by the hour, the arithmetic is easy and *they* should do it:

> "You've got 14 consultants. You said your internal meetings run about 20% over because there's no agenda. At €150/hr billable that's roughly [X] hours a week of non-billable drift. Our price for the whole team is €350 a month. You don't need me to do the rest of that sum."

Note this is buyer-stated arithmetic, not a vendor ROI deck — which is how it survives Gong's −27% ROI finding.

### "Send me a proposal"

The mechanic is right — don't send a document into a void — but the published scripts are American hard-sell register and will read as pushy in Northern Europe. Keep the mechanic, drop the register. "Is that fair?" is the one phrase from them that Gong's data independently supports and it travels fine.

**⭐ At small ACV there's a better answer than any script: don't write proposals.** A proposal exists because enterprise procurement demands one. Replace it with a one-screen email — price, seats, start date, cancellation terms, payment link — sent while still on the call. "Send me a proposal" is often a genuine request for **something forwardable to a partner**, and six lines forward better than a PDF.

### Silence and ghosting

**⭐ Kazanjy's off-ramp email, verbatim and in full — the best break-up for a founder:**

> "We've missed a couple follow-up appointments. While I think that our solution would be extremely valuable to your organization, as we agreed that <some citation of ROI that is specific to them>, I don't want to occupy your time if it's unlikely we'll be able to help you out. But I'd really like to know so I can prioritize my time effectively, and also not bother you unnecessarily. Are you still interested in working toward becoming a customer? Is now a bad time? Or does it turn out that this is something that doesn't make sense for <their company name> after all? I can take the truth, but I would like to have clarity!"

The diagnostic for when to send it:

> "If you aren't seeing specific movement forward, and instead you're seeing a series of broken 'micro-contracts' on the part of the prospect, the deal is probably on its way down."

**Chris Voss's one-liner**, as subject line and body, nothing else added:

> "Have you given up on this project?"

> "When you ask 'Have you given up on this?,' you're practically begging them to refute you, and that refute opens the floodgates, while giving them the feeling they're in control."

**Voss's own caution:** it only works where there was genuine prior engagement — used as an opener it's manipulation. Use Kazanjy's as the default (founder-voiced, honest, offers three named exits, doesn't feel like a technique) and Voss's as the shorter second follow-up.

### Negotiation

**Never negotiate over email** (Gong, n=37,671 opportunities): pricing handled by **email only 5% win rate** · **phone only 15%** · **phone + email 35%**. Win rate peaks at 31% when pricing has been discussed but negotiation hasn't started, and falls to 12% as email negotiation increases.

Move it to a call, verbatim:

> "Totally makes sense. Let's hop on a quick call to align pricing with your budget plus answer any questions. I'm sure we can knock this out in 7-9 minutes tops. Does tomorrow at 4 PM work?"

Treat *any* buyer ask — payment terms, seat minimums — as negotiation beginning, and pivot to a call.

**Apologise *during* the negotiation, not *for* it:**

> "I'm sorry. I want to offer you that number – if it were up to me, I would. But that discount simply isn't doable."

⚠️ The widely-cited *"How can I ask my boss to give you 3 months free AND a 10% discount?"* **does not work for a solo founder** — you have no boss, and it invites "you *are* the boss". Substitute the *policy* ("I only have one price; if I discount for you I have to do it for everyone, and I'd rather keep the price honest") or the *trade* (see `pricing.md`).

**Never say "list price"** — it extends cycles by **19%**, and using it in the first two calls **triples** the time spent discussing price. Say *"our price is X."*
