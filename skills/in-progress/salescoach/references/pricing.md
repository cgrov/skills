# Pricing posture for the first customers

⚠️ secondary or directional · 🚩 conflict or scale mismatch.

---

## 1. The default: hold the price

> **"If a SaaS purchase can go on a credit card, you don't need to discount."** — Jason Lemkin

That single rule decides the posture for most founder-led deals. Discounting expectations arrive as ACV rises and procurement gets involved. Below that line there's no procurement, no negotiation bonus, and no champion who needs a "win" to show internally. **Hold the price, publish it, and don't negotiate the per-unit number.**

## 2. Why, empirically

ProfitWell / Paddle, **6,000+ companies and 600,000 subscribers**:

> "Discounted customers have just over double the churn rate as those who weren't given a discount."

> "As the discount goes up, the churn rate goes up as well, because customers receiving really big discounts just weren't ready to be customers but were enticed by that discount."

> "customers who receive heavy discounts have simply been trained to devalue the product or just weren't the right customers in the first place."

LTV: *"The average difference in lifetime value was upwards of 32.41% less"* for aggressively-discounted acquisition.

⚠️ **The sample behind that split is small — 55 companies vs 33.** Cite the direction with confidence; treat "32.41%" as a precise-looking number resting on 88 companies.

Worked example from the same source: a 20% discount on a $500/mo customer with $6,000 CAC pushed payback from **12 months to 15**.

If you must discount, Paddle's conditions: **discrete** (no banners on the site) · **segmented** (only price-sensitive prospects) · **time-boxed** — *"never offer lifetime discounts; establish expiration dates."*

## 3. The counter-school, and how to resolve it

Lemkin also says, of getting the first ten: offer free trials or steep discounts to lower the barrier, while **learning from them and turning them into advocates**. EchoSign's early customers were *"beer money"* — barely paid the bills, and the foundation for everything after.

Design-partner structures circulating under his name ⚠️ (several SaaStr "Dear SaaStr" answers are AI-generated from his corpus — treat the percentages as plausible guidance, not measured findings): 30–50% off for 12–24 months; or lock a 40% discount for 3 years in exchange for quarterly feedback and a case study; keep the cohort *"small (3–5 max)"*; *"Formalize the terms in a simple agreement"*; don't undervalue the product.

**⭐ The two schools are arguing about different currencies.** "Never discount" protects revenue quality and retention. "The first ten are for learning" buys information and proof.

Do the arithmetic for the founder's actual numbers. On a €3,000/year account, a 40% discount saves the customer €1,200/year — **not enough to be the reason a firm says yes or no, but enough to anchor them low permanently and (per ProfitWell) roughly double their churn probability.**

**What the founder actually needs from the first ten is not cheapness — it's named references and a repeatable pitch.** Both are purchasable with things that cost nothing: attention, roadmap influence, direct founder access.

**The posture: hold list price for all ten. Trade non-price value for commitments. Discount only for cash timing, never for a signature.**

## 4. The trade menu

Kazanjy's levers, verbatim: **price per unit, total cost, contract duration, and payment terms.** For each discount request, counter by asking for **more seats, a longer contract, or prepayment**.

> "Start with inflated pricing in anticipation of being negotiated down, so that where you end up still provides an economically viable deal"

> For shorter contracts: "make the per-time price for that duration at least 2–3x what it would be the full year"

> "raise your pricing when the prospect wants to split up payments (biannually, quarterly, monthly)"

> "you should always prioritize getting more money in the door up front" · **"Cash is king for early-stage companies"**

| Ask for | Why it's worth it |
|---|---|
| **Annual prepay** | Cash now, and annual customers churn less |
| **Named public logo + website listing** | Kills the "who else uses this?" objection for the next nine deals |
| **A written case study** | The single hardest asset to get later |
| **A reference-call commitment** — specify the number, e.g. two in twelve months | Turns one customer into leverage on many |
| **All the seats, not a sub-team** | Prevents the pilot-forever pattern and raises switching cost |
| **A faster decision date** | Cycle time is a solo founder's scarcest resource |
| **Intros to two peer firms** | Highest ROI in a tight market, and almost nobody asks |

**The line, every time:**

> "I can do that. What I'd need in return is [X]. Does that work?"

Never "yes" alone. Never "no" alone. Always the trade.

## 5. Annual prepay

⚠️ **This is the weakest-sourced item in the whole corpus.** No primary benchmark for the discount percentage could be found. *"10–20% is standard"* and *"annual customers churn ~30% less"* are **commonly repeated, unverified**. **"Two months free" = 16.7%** is *arithmetic and convention*, not research — 12 months priced as 10.

**Use "two months free on annual".** It's the most legible offer in SaaS, needs no justification, collects the year on day one instead of across twelve billing events, and — critically — it's a discount for **cash timing**, the one kind ProfitWell's data doesn't indict.

**Never offer both an annual discount and a negotiated per-unit discount to the same customer.**

## 6. Grandfathering — your best legitimate urgency

> "the best pricing changes happen gradually, with plenty of notice, and with a clear narrative around increased value. Grandfather existing customers or offer them meaningful choices to maintain goodwill." — Kyle Poyar

Practices: **60–90 days' notice** for significant changes; for increases beyond 50%, *"stair-step them so they gradually move up to the new rates rather than swallowing it all at once."*

His exception, worth knowing: *"Unlike traditional SaaS, you can't just grandfather legacy users [if] those customers are unprofitable and you're racing toward $10 million ARR."*

**⭐ Grandfathering is cheap at ten customers and expensive at five hundred. Promise it out loud and use it as a closing asset, not as a concession made later under pressure:**

> "You'll keep this price for as long as you're a customer. I'll raise prices for new customers — I won't raise them on you."

That converts "we should wait and see" into "we should do it now" **without a fake deadline** — which is exactly the shape Dixon's data says urgency must take. It costs nothing at this stage and it's the strongest honest urgency device available.

## 7. When to say the number

From `conversations.md`, repeated here because founders get it wrong in the same direction every time:

- **Pricing discussed on the first call: 42% win rate.** Second call 32%, third 15%, **never mentioned 5%.** (n=11,331 opportunities)
- Star reps raise it about **three-quarters of the way through** the conversation. On a 22-minute call that's minute 16–17. 🚩 Gong publishes both "38–46 minutes" and "40–49 minutes" across different articles — the ratio is the transferable form, not the clock time.
- **Unsuccessful demos have no consistent pricing window at all**, and spend **8% more** time on price.
- **3–4 buyer pricing questions** correlates with the highest win rates.
- **Never say "list price"** — extends cycles 19%.
- **Price ≠ budget.** State your price. Don't interrogate their budget — doing so on call one extends the interaction from two calls to four.

**Everything here argues harder at a small price.** The reason to delay price in enterprise is that value must first exceed a scary number. A small number isn't scary. Waiting manufactures suspense around something that would have *relieved* the buyer fifteen minutes earlier.

## 8. Finding out what they'd pay

**Don't run Van Westendorp at n=10** — it needs a distribution to plot, and it's criticised for hypothetical bias anyway.

**Run the ladder conversationally in every discovery call and log the answers to `learned.md`.** Ramanujam's version, three questions asked progressively:

- What price would feel **acceptable** for this?
- At what price does it start to feel **expensive** — not out of the question, but you'd have to think about it?
- At what price is it **prohibitive** — you wouldn't consider it?

And Poyar's two-question version, which is often better because it's about their behaviour rather than their opinion:

> "How are you solving this today? How much does that cost you?"

Ramanujam's floor, verbatim:

> "Talk to at least one person. Most companies are not even doing that."

A usable picture emerges after ~20 conversations, for zero cost, and it arrives *before* the founder has to quote. Gong's data cuts the same way — pricing discussed early correlates with the highest win rates — so asking these questions is both research and selling.

For reference, the full **Van Westendorp** four questions, if the founder ever has the volume for it:

1. At what price would you consider the product **so expensive that you would not consider buying it**?
2. At what price would you consider it priced **so low that you would feel the quality couldn't be very good**?
3. At what price would you consider it **starting to get expensive** — not out of the question, but you'd have to give some thought to buying it?
4. At what price would you consider it **a bargain — a great buy for the money**?

One question per screen, so respondents can't back-adjust for consistency.
